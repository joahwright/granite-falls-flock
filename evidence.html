Cleaner excerpts from Granite Falls public records, paired with context explaining why each document matters.

Transparency

Officials repeatedly discussed public-records exposure.

The emails below document concern about whether Granite Falls would receive Flock-related public-records requests. In a September 2026 meeting, City Manager Jeff Balentine told this project that those concerns were raised to protect citizen ALPR data from being obtained through records requests. Washington’s ESSB 6002 has since exempted ALPR data from public inspection and copying under the Public Records Act.

Context and project position
Balentine’s explanation provides important context for the correspondence. It does not change this project’s policy concern: a surveillance program that officials believe must operate under fear of a public-records request is a poor foundation for transparent public oversight. The emails establish what officials wrote; they do not by themselves prove unlawful withholding of any particular request.
Proposed exemption

Counsel circulated a legislative template.

City counsel circulated a template asking Washington legislators to create a specific Public Records Act exemption for ALPR data. The subsequent thread discusses legislative advocacy and Flock's position that the agency, not Flock, owns the data.

Claimed public-safety uses

The record also contains the City's case for Flock.

Chief Tom Dalton supplied examples that he said showed Flock assisting with a fatal hit-and-run investigation, a mental-health response, search-and-rescue and an illegal-dumping investigation. Including the City's claimed benefits is necessary to present the record fairly.

Public-records administration

The City asked Flock for a technical solution.

Deputy City Manager Jeff Balentine asked Flock Legal about a cloud-based portal that could reduce administrative burden and potential penalties when producing Flock records. Flock responded that product development was already addressing some of the issues.

Renewal + policy advocacy

Police leadership made renewal a priority and pushed for broader rules.

Council disagreement

Opposition existed inside City government too.

Councilmember Bruce Straughn wrote that he opposed the original Flock contract and remained opposed. He later asked for another Council discussion and a legal interpretation of developing court rulings.

Scale + physical maintenance

The cameras were collecting tens of thousands of reads.

A five-day dashboard sample from Feb. 13–17, 2025 covers five active cameras and shows 40,470 total vehicle reads and 28,515 unique vehicle reads. Separate 2025 correspondence shows city staff arranging to trim a tree that was blocking a 100th Street Flock camera.

Final City production

Installment 6 closes the City file — not the operational record.

City Clerk Darla Wilkins described Installment 6 as the City’s sixth and final production and stated that the City had provided all responsive records currently in its possession. The City identified request items 5, 6, 7, 8 and 10 as matters for the Snohomish County Sheriff’s Office.

Installment 6 adds Council packets, police activity reports and the City’s 2026 ALPR compliance presentations. It also adds additional examples in which officials credited Flock with investigative value. It does not contain the requested SCSO query-by-query audit, search and access logs.

Records-custody distinction
The City’s production being complete does not establish that the operational records do not exist. SCSO request K227577-050426 remains open for those categories, with the next installment currently estimated for Nov. 23, 2026.
SCSO answers

The direct answers resolve some questions and create others.

High-school camera

Chief Tom Dalton stated that the Penny Avenue/100th Street camera was never disabled or physically moved. He said it was re-aimed slightly north around May 1, 2026, in response to public-perception concerns and to better align with the intent of SB 6002, and that the change reduced some data collection. He also stated that City and SCSO counsel believe the placement complies with the statute. That is the agencies’ legal position, not a court or Attorney General finding.

Audit trail

Dalton stated that SCSO’s audit trail records the user, deputy name, networks searched, number of devices, search timeframe, plate, reason, case number, filters, search time and search type, and that the records are retained indefinitely. He also said Granite Falls-specific organizational audits are not scheduled because SCSO searches are performed through the SCSO account.

False hits and misreads

Dalton answered “Not tracked” when asked whether SCSO tracks false hits, plate misreads or alerts involving the wrong vehicle. The records currently produced therefore do not provide a Granite Falls false-positive or plate-misread rate.

System changes + anonymized data

Dalton stated that Flock had not asked for a change affecting sharing and that he was not familiar with that approval process. He also stated that he was not familiar with the Granite Falls contract and did not know whether Flock is deriving anonymized data from Granite Falls captures.

Annual-audit question remains open
RCW 10.130.080 requires each agency operating or accessing an ALPR system to maintain specified audit-trail data and conduct an internal audit at least annually. The remaining factual question is how Granite Falls-related searches are incorporated into an annual SCSO audit and how the City documents compliance for the system it funds and contracts for.

Public-records process

Nine questions were initially routed as a PRA request, then answered directly.

On Sept. 11, Chief Dalton wrote that he was considering the nine-question email a public-disclosure request and was forwarding it for dissemination. Snohomish County later opened request K252082-091826 using the text of the questions.

Wright objected that the email was seeking answers rather than identifiable public records, citing RCW 42.56.080 and Snohomish County’s published public-records standards. Dalton later sent direct substantive answers to the questions. This chronology is presented as a process issue; it does not, by itself, establish intentional obstruction or a legal violation.

Effectiveness evidence

The file contains successful examples, but not a complete performance evaluation.

The produced record contains several incidents officials described as successful Flock uses, including the earlier four-case list, the McDaniel’s Hardware burglary and the January 2026 AED-theft investigation. A 2025 police report also attributed lower auto theft to a combination of pursuit-law changes, Flock and community outreach.

What the production reviewed for this project does not provide is a systematic local denominator: no all-search outcome analysis, no Granite Falls false-positive rate, no plate-misread rate, and no defined methodology comparing Flock-assisted outcomes with unsuccessful or inaccurate searches. SCSO’s direct response that false hits and misreads are “not tracked” makes that limitation more concrete.

What can be concluded from the record
The records document examples where officials say Flock was useful. They do not currently support an objective Granite Falls-wide effectiveness rate or an error-rate estimate. The open SCSO records request may add information needed for a fuller evaluation.
