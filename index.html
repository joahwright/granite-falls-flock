01 / Compliance questions

Start with the records.

Two issues should be resolved before any renewal decision: current statutory compliance and reconciliation of the final installed camera locations with the City's permitting record. The City's records show that camera locations changed during deployment.

What the law requires / what the record shows This section separates the statutory rule from the City's documents. Where the existing record does not prove actual noncompliance, the site says so and identifies what the City should document before renewal.

Retention: 30 days in the contract / 21 days in state law

The executed Master Services Agreement states that Flock's standard retention period is a rolling 30 days unless otherwise stated on the Order Form. Washington's 2026 ALPR law generally limits agency ALPR-data retention to 21 days, subject to listed exceptions. The existing contract language therefore creates a compliance question; the City should document the actual Granite Falls retention configuration and purge schedule before renewal.

Final deployment changed from the original planning record

Flock deployment records show that camera locations changed during implementation. Camera #03 was relocated during deployment and was ultimately installed at Robe Menzel Road / South Granite Avenue on March 31, 2025. On April 1, 2025, Flock reported seven total locations, seven cameras LIVE, and implementation 100% complete.

The City should reconcile the final seven installed locations with ROW Permit 2024-019 and identify any subsequent location approvals, amendments, or other authorization covering the changed installations.

02 / What was purchased

More than a plate camera.

The City's executed order includes FlockOS and seven Falcon cameras. The attached product description lists a broader set of FlockOS capabilities available through the platform. That does not by itself establish how often, or whether, Granite Falls has used each capability.

One listed feature is what Flock itself calls “Vehicle Fingerprint Search.” In the contract, that means searching captured vehicles by characteristics such as type, make, color, plate state, missing or covered plates, and other visual features. It is not a biometric fingerprint search.

The Master Services Agreement gives Flock certain rights to access, preserve, or disclose footage under specified circumstances, but the agreement itself does not appear to establish a public-facing audit regime for Granite Falls. Washington's 2026 ALPR law now requires agencies to retain audit-trail data documenting access, searches, exports, downloads, and sharing, and to conduct an internal audit at least annually.

03 / Why the guardrails matter

Collected first. Searched later.

The central concern is not that every photograph of a license plate is automatically an unconstitutional search. It is what becomes possible when routine observations of ordinary drivers are accumulated into a searchable record of movement before those drivers are suspected of any crime.

Investigation can begin after the database already exists A traditional investigation starts with an event, a suspect or articulable evidence and works outward. A networked ALPR system can reverse that sequence: movement is collected continuously, and an authorized user decides later whose history to search.

That distinction matters because abuse has already occurred. Police officers around the country have reportedly used license-plate reader systems to search for romantic partners, former partners and other people for personal reasons. These cases do not prove Granite Falls officers have misused Flock. They prove that insider misuse is a demonstrated risk that policy, auditing and technical controls have to be designed around.

The constitutional question is therefore larger than whether a plate is visible from a public road. Modern surveillance systems can aggregate thousands of individually ordinary observations into a revealing map of a person's life. Courts and legal scholars continue to wrestle with where persistent, searchable location surveillance crosses the line from observation into an unreasonable government search.

The policy question for Granite Falls is simpler: how much information about people who are not suspected of crimes should government collect in advance, and how much confidence should residents be required to place in every future officer, administrator, vendor and agency that can touch that system?

04 / Local control

Local camera. Outside reach.

Granite Falls can write a local policy. That does not automatically guarantee that locally collected data stays within the boundaries residents or councilmembers think they approved. University of Washington researchers reviewing Flock audit records found apparent U.S. Border Patrol searches of data from at least ten Washington police departments that had not explicitly authorized Border Patrol searches of their network data.

Immigration enforcement is one documented use, and Washington law places specific limits on local assistance with civil immigration enforcement. But the larger concern crosses partisan lines: municipal surveillance data can become reachable by agencies beyond the municipality that collected it. The same structural problem exists whether the outside user is Border Patrol, another police department or a future federal agency pursuing a different policy.

UW researchers also found discrepancies between Flock audits and network-sharing documentation that made it impossible for independent researchers to determine the complete scope of access in the systems they reviewed. The report further notes that Flock has declined to allow independent security analysts to test its devices.

The issue is control If Granite Falls says its data may be used only for defined purposes, residents should be able to verify that the technology actually enforces those boundaries — not merely trust that settings, vendor architecture and user-entered search reasons will always work as intended.

05 / Broader concern

Public power. Private infrastructure.

Flock is not simply selling a camera bolted to a pole. It operates the software, network and infrastructure through which government agencies can search and share location data. That places a significant public-surveillance function inside a privately owned technology company.

Andreessen Horowitz is a major Flock investor; Reuters reported that the firm led a $275 million Flock funding round in 2025, its third investment in the company. Andreessen Horowitz also lists Israeli cyber-intelligence company Toka in its portfolio.

Reporting by Haaretz, based on Toka materials, described technology capable of accessing networked security cameras and altering live or recorded video feeds while minimizing forensic traces. There is no evidence presented here that Toka has accessed Granite Falls or Flock systems. The concern is concentration: the same investment ecosystem backs a company expanding government surveillance infrastructure and a company developing tools capable of covertly compromising surveillance infrastructure.

That connection is not the legal case against Granite Falls' current deployment. It is a reason to ask a broader democratic question: how much surveillance capacity should local government outsource to privately financed systems whose technology, ownership and capabilities extend far beyond one city's contract?

The operative decision

Require a public renewal vote.

The legal questions and the broader concerns point to the same democratic remedy: do not allow renewal to happen by inertia. The Flock agreement renews unless either party gives notice of non-renewal at least 30 days before the end of the current term. The exact deadline depends on the system's installation-and-validation start date.

The concrete ask: put the Flock contract on a public council agenda; document actual compliance with Washington's current retention, access, sharing and audit requirements; publicly identify the final seven operating camera locations and the authorization covering each installation; and require every councilmember to cast a recorded vote on renewal.

Residents do not need to agree on every privacy, immigration, policing or technology question to agree on that. A surveillance system this consequential should continue only after an open debate and an accountable vote.
