How much should government remember?

Granite Falls has seven Flock Safety automated license plate readers. Washington now places new limits on how ALPR systems may be used, retained, shared and audited. The question for Granite Falls is broader: whether a continuously searchable record of ordinary vehicle movements should remain part of local policing as the platform behind those cameras continues to evolve.

02 / The law caught up

For now.

Washington enacted ESSB 6002 in 2026 after ALPR systems had already spread across the state. The law created limits on retention, purposes, sharing, sensitive locations, audit trails and traffic stops. At the August 19 Granite Falls briefing, SCSO represented that the program had been brought into compliance with the new statute. This site therefore does not allege that Granite Falls is currently using the old 30-day retention setting.

RetentionALPR data generally may not be retained longer than 21 days, subject to statutory exceptions.
Search purposesThe statute limits law-enforcement use to specified public-safety and investigative purposes.
Positive matchAn ALPR match by itself does not create reasonable suspicion for a vehicle stop; verification or independent reasonable suspicion is required.
SharingDirect access is restricted to authorized Washington state or local agencies, with vendor controls required against unauthorized access.
Audit trailAgencies must log access, queries, purposes, case/call information where applicable, exports, downloads and sharing.
Annual auditAgencies must review system use and compliance with retention, purging and sharing requirements at least annually.

03 / Retention

Why keep 21 days?

The 2024 Granite Falls contract used Flock's then-standard 30-day retention language. Washington later imposed a general 21-day maximum. Then, on August 13, 2026, Flock announced a new 7-day recommended/default retention period, saying its analysis showed that more than 90% of searches without a complete plate occur within one week.

Washington permits

Up to 21 days for ordinary ALPR data, subject to the statute's listed exceptions.

Flock recommends

Seven days by default, with evidence preserved when an active investigation and the law require it.

Compliance explains why 21 days is allowed. It does not explain why Granite Falls needs 21 days.

If seven days meets Flock's own stated operational need for the overwhelming majority of searches, keeping three weeks of routine travel records deserves a separate local justification. The burden should be to explain the additional retention, not to assume it simply because state law permits it.

04 / Regulatory lag

Law moves slower than software.

A statute is written at a point in time. A software platform is not. Flock can change product defaults, add safeguards, introduce new search tools, connect additional sensors and launch new products without replacing the pole already standing beside the road. Washington law now adds an important limit: Flock may not make system changes that affect sharing permissions without the authorized Washington agency's knowledge and explicit consent.

That does not mean Flock is evading Washington law. It means regulation starts from a disadvantage: the capability usually exists before lawmakers and courts have enough experience with it to draw durable boundaries. Product development is continuous; legislation and litigation are not.

That matters because Granite Falls is not only deciding whether to keep seven pieces of hardware. It is deciding whether to remain connected to an evolving private surveillance platform whose future capabilities can change without a new pole, a new road permit or the same level of public debate that accompanied the original deployment.

June 2026
Chatrie v. United States

The U.S. Supreme Court held that police conducted a Fourth Amendment search when they obtained Okello Chatrie's cell-phone location history from Google through a geofence process. The case reached the Court years after geofence warrants had already become an investigative tool, illustrating how constitutional rules can develop after location-surveillance technology is already in use.

Neither example proves that Granite Falls' Flock system is unlawful. They show why "we comply with today's law" is not a complete answer to a technology whose capabilities and legal boundaries are still developing.

05 / A bipartisan principle

Limiting government power is not partisan.

People can disagree sharply about policing, immigration, firearms, reproductive rights and federal power while agreeing on a simpler principle: government surveillance authority should have defined limits that do not depend on trusting every future official who inherits the system.

39–10Washington Senate vote on ESSB 6002
84–10Washington House vote on ESSB 6002

The Legislature's overwhelming vote does not prove that every Washingtonian agrees on Flock. It does show broad political recognition that location-surveillance technology requires guardrails. The remaining local question is whether Granite Falls should merely stay inside those guardrails or choose not to maintain the system at all.

You do not have to distrust police to limit government power.

07 / The school-area question

Camera #07 deserves an answer.

City and Flock location materials identify camera #07 as the eastbound 100th St NE / Penny Ave installation in the Granite Falls High School area. The City's ROW permit packet includes the location sheet for that camera.

ESSB 6002 makes it unlawful for an agency to collect ALPR data on the premises, immediate surroundings, or access to or from elementary and secondary schools and other listed sensitive facilities. The question therefore turns on more than the pole's street address: it depends on where #07 is pointed and what roadway or school-access traffic its collection area actually captures.

The City's permit packet already documents the camera's placement and eastbound collection geometry, and that material is reproduced in the records linked below. The remaining question is whether the documented collection area falls within ESSB 6002's prohibition on collecting ALPR data on the premises, immediate surroundings, or access to or from a secondary school.

08 / What the numbers show

Correlation is not causation.

SCSO's August 19 presentation pointed to Granite Falls' low crime during the Flock period as evidence of the system's value. But the City's own police reports show that several of the same trends were already underway before the cameras came online.

In the City's January 2025 annual report, SCSO reported that auto theft had already declined during 2024 and noted that Granite Falls' most prolific vehicle thief had been convicted and sent to prison for seven years. The same report said theft of motor-vehicle parts was continuing to decline and attributed that trend to changes in state law. Flock was still described in future tense: the cameras were expected to come online in February 2025 and were anticipated to help further reduce vehicle theft.

The January 2025 monthly report makes the timing even clearer. Before Flock installation began on February 13, deputies recorded 451 calls for service — about 100 fewer than the prior January and lower than January 2023. That month Granite Falls recorded one auto-theft case and recovered three stolen vehicles without the Flock network yet operating.

SCSO's own 2025 annual summary later described auto theft as a continuing downward trend supported by multiple factors, including legislative changes, ALPR technology and community outreach. That is a materially narrower claim than treating low crime during the Flock period as proof that Flock caused the decline.

Flock can produce useful leads in individual investigations. The local record supports that. What the record does not establish is that Granite Falls' broader crime trends were caused by seven ALPR cameras. Those are different claims, and they should not be presented as interchangeable.

The operative decision

Do not renew by inertia.

Granite Falls does not need to prove that SCSO is breaking the law before deciding not to renew Flock. The Council's job is broader than checking a compliance box: it has to decide whether the investigative value is worth maintaining a system that routinely records ordinary vehicle movements and depends on a private platform whose capabilities will continue to change.

While the system remains active, Granite Falls should move to Flock's seven-day retention recommendation unless a shorter lawful period is chosen, publicly resolve the #07 school-area question, publish meaningful audit information, and put continuation of the contract on a public council agenda.

This site's position is straightforward: Granite Falls should not renew the Flock contract. If the Council disagrees, continuation should occur only after an open debate and a recorded vote in which the public-safety benefit and the surveillance cost are both acknowledged.
