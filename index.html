02 / Current compliance

Verify the configuration.

The strongest current questions are no longer about whether the seventh camera existed. They are whether the actual operating configuration complies with Washington's new rules on retention, restricted collection locations, access, sharing and audit trails.

Contract language

The 2024 Master Services Agreement describes Flock's standard footage retention as a rolling 30 days, unless otherwise stated on the Order Form.

Current Washington law

ESSB 6002 generally limits ALPR-data retention to 21 days, subject to statutory exceptions. The operative question is the system's current configured purge schedule, not the old contract default standing alone.

What should be documented before renewal The City and SCSO should be able to identify the current retention setting; the current sharing configuration; the final camera locations and collection areas; the applicable user-access controls; and the audit records required by state law.

04 / Contracted capability

Capability is not authority.

The City's executed order includes FlockOS and seven Falcon cameras. Its product-description table lists capabilities including state and nationwide license-plate lookup, time-and-location searches, law-enforcement network access, vehicle-characteristic searching, custom hot lists and real-time alerts.

Important distinction A capability listed in the 2024 contract does not prove that Granite Falls officers used it, and it does not establish that the capability remains legally available for unrestricted use after ESSB 6002. Contracted capability and current authorized use are separate questions.

No facial recognition required

A Falcon camera captures a plate and vehicle characteristics. That observation can become person-linked when authorized law-enforcement databases associate the plate with vehicle-registration information.

The camera itself should not be described as identifying the driver by name. The privacy issue is the combined system: plate observation + searchable movement history + other government databases.

05 / Current rules

Searches now have guardrails.

The operative framework is no longer just the 2024 contract and draft policy that accompanied it. Washington's 2026 statute requires detailed audit trails and annual internal audits, and SCSO now publishes a current dedicated ALPR policy. The website therefore distinguishes the vendor's available features from the uses that current law and policy authorize.

Authorized comparisonsFor criminal investigations, Washington law limits ALPR comparisons to specified government databases or vehicles that are stolen; associated with a missing or endangered person; registered to someone with an outstanding felony or gross-misdemeanor warrant; or related to a felony or gross misdemeanor.
Positive matchA positive ALPR match alone does not create reasonable suspicion for a traffic stop. The officer must develop independent reasonable suspicion or perform the confirmation required by the statute.
Audit trailState law requires records showing who accessed or searched the system, when, the query elements, the specific purpose, the associated case/call number where applicable, camera location, and records of exports, downloads and sharing.
Vendor audit dataAgencies must obtain and retain vendor-generated audit trail data required by the statute.
Annual reviewEach agency operating or accessing an ALPR system must conduct an internal audit at least annually for access/use and compliance with retention, purging and sharing requirements.
Current SCSO policySCSO's current ALPR policy is now publicly posted. It should be read together with ESSB 6002 when evaluating what Granite Falls deputies may do today.
01Plate readA passing vehicle is detected and recorded by an ALPR camera.
02Search / comparisonAn authorized query or hot-list comparison is performed.
03Alert / resultThe system returns a possible match or responsive record.
04VerificationA system result is not the same thing as independently confirmed probable cause.
05OutcomeA verified lead may contribute to a stop, recovery, arrest or other investigation.

This distinction matters when reading usage statistics. Plate reads, searches, alerts, matches, stops, arrests and prosecutions are different measurements. They should not be collapsed into a single number that implies every camera read was an officer search or every alert produced an arrest.

06 / Why the guardrails matter

Collected first. Searched later.

The central concern is not that every photograph of a license plate is automatically an unconstitutional search. It is what becomes possible when routine observations of ordinary drivers are accumulated into a searchable record of movement before those drivers are suspected of a crime.

Investigation can begin after the database already exists A networked ALPR system can preserve observations before a particular person or vehicle becomes the subject of an investigation. That is why retention limits, access controls, query documentation and audits matter even when each individual plate was visible from a public road.

Documented misuse elsewhere does not prove misuse in Granite Falls. It demonstrates why technical controls and independent auditing matter. The appropriate question is not whether every authorized user will act improperly; it is whether the system is designed so misuse can be prevented, detected and reconstructed when it occurs.

07 / Sharing & protected locations

Local system. Statewide rules.

Historical reporting on Flock networks in Washington showed why outside access became a major policy issue. Washington has since imposed new restrictions on ALPR data sharing, and current SCSO policy must be evaluated under those rules. The responsible question now is whether Granite Falls' actual sharing settings and audit logs match the limits required by current law and policy.

The issue is verifiable control Local policy should be reflected in actual system permissions and auditable records. A written rule is strongest when the technical configuration and audit trail independently demonstrate that unauthorized outside access is blocked and detectable.

ESSB 6002 also restricts ALPR collection at specified sensitive locations, including elementary and secondary schools and their immediate surroundings/access routes. Granite Falls' deployment records include a school-adjacent location in the 100th Street / high-school area. The legal question should therefore be framed around the camera's present location, orientation and actual collection area, rather than declaring a violation without evidence about the current field of view or any post-2026 change.

08 / Broader concern

Public power. Private infrastructure.

Flock is not simply selling a camera bolted to a pole. It operates the software, network and infrastructure through which government agencies can search and share location data. That places a significant public-surveillance function inside a privately owned technology company.

Andreessen Horowitz is a major Flock investor; Reuters reported that the firm led a $275 million Flock funding round in 2025, its third investment in the company. Andreessen Horowitz also lists Israeli cyber-intelligence company Toka in its portfolio.

Reporting by Haaretz, based on Toka materials, described technology capable of accessing networked security cameras and altering live or recorded video feeds while minimizing forensic traces. There is no evidence presented here that Toka has accessed Granite Falls or Flock systems. The concern is concentration: the same investment ecosystem backs a company expanding government surveillance infrastructure and a company developing tools capable of covertly compromising surveillance infrastructure.

That connection is not the legal case against Granite Falls' current deployment. It is a reason to ask a broader democratic question: how much surveillance capacity should local government outsource to privately financed systems whose technology, ownership and capabilities extend far beyond one city's contract?

The operative decision

Require a public renewal vote.

The legal questions and the broader concerns point to the same democratic remedy: do not allow renewal to happen by inertia. The Flock agreement renews unless either party gives notice of non-renewal at least 30 days before the end of the current term. The exact deadline depends on the system's installation-and-validation start date.

The concrete ask: document the current 21-day retention configuration and purge process; publish or summarize the required audit results; identify the current sharing settings and final seven camera locations/collection areas; address the school-location restriction; and require every councilmember to cast a recorded vote before renewal.

Residents do not need to agree on every privacy, immigration, policing or technology question to agree on that. A surveillance system this consequential should continue only after an open debate and an accountable vote.
